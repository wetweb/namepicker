Victorian baby names

Updated 24 November 2026

Old-fashioned names are having a moment, and the Victorians had some of the best. In the 19th century people brought back long-forgotten Old English names, named babies after the royal family, and borrowed heroines from the bestselling novels and poems of the day.

Tap any name to read its full meaning and origin.

Royal names

  • Victoria (Girl): Latin for “victory”. Queen Victoria, who reigned for 63 years, made it hugely popular.
  • Albert (Boy): “Noble” and “bright”. Queen Victoria’s husband, Prince Albert, gave it a big revival.
  • Alexandrina (Girl): A longer form of Alexandra, and Queen Victoria’s own first name.
  • Alberta (Girl): A feminine form of Albert. A Canadian province is named after one of Queen Victoria’s daughters.
  • Adelaide (Girl): Roughly “noble sort”. The Australian city honours a 19th-century British queen.

From Victorian novels and poems

  • Alice (Girl): From an Old French short form of Adelaide. Lewis Carroll’s “Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland” made it famous worldwide.
  • Charlotte (Girl): A French feminine form of Charles. Charlotte Brontë wrote “Jane Eyre”.
  • Maud (Girl): A medieval form of Matilda, revived after Tennyson’s poem “Maud”.
  • Enid (Girl): Probably from Welsh for “soul, life”. Tennyson’s poem about faithful Enid made it popular.
  • Elaine (Girl): An Old French form of Helen. Tennyson’s Arthurian poems made it popular in the 19th century.
  • Ida (Girl): Revived in the 19th century, helped by Tennyson’s poem “The Princess”.
  • Mabel (Girl): Meaning “lovable”. The novel “The Heir of Redclyffe” brought it back into use.
  • Estella (Girl): Borne by Estella Havisham in Dickens’ “Great Expectations”.
  • Oliver (Boy): Probably linked to the olive tree. Dickens’ “Oliver Twist” made it famous.
  • Pip (Girl or boy): A pet form of Philip or Philippa, and the hero of Dickens’ “Great Expectations”.
  • Ebenezer (Boy): Hebrew for “stone of help”. Dickens chose it for Ebenezer Scrooge in “A Christmas Carol”.

Old names the Victorians brought back

These go back to Anglo-Saxon or medieval England and were revived in the 19th century.

  • Edith (Girl): Old English for “rich in war”, popular among Anglo-Saxon royalty.
  • Audrey (Girl): A medieval pet form of a name meaning “noble strength”. Audrey Hepburn is its best-known bearer.
  • Amy (Girl): From Old French for “loved”. It came back into fashion in the 19th century.
  • Marjorie (Girl): A medieval variant of Margery, influenced by the herb marjoram.
  • Edwin (Boy): Old English for “rich friend”, borne by a 7th-century king of Northumbria.
  • Harold (Boy): Old English for “army ruler”. King Harold II died at the Battle of Hastings in 1066.
  • Herbert (Boy): Germanic for “bright army”, brought to England by the Normans.
  • Arnold (Boy): From a Germanic name meaning “eagle power”.
  • Guy (Boy): Rare after Guy Fawkes’ plot of 1605, then revived in the 19th century.
  • Milo (Boy): The Germanic and Latin form of Miles, revived in English in the 19th century.
  • Cecil (Boy): Popular in the 19th century in honour of the powerful Cecil family of England.

New in the 19th century

Flower and word names first caught on with the Victorians.

  • Ethel (Girl): Coined in the 19th century from Old English names beginning with “æthel” (noble).
  • Daisy (Girl): From Old English for “day’s eye”, first used as a name in the 19th century.
  • Violet (Girl): From the purple flower, used as a name since the 19th century.
  • Hazel (Girl): From the hazel tree. Popular in the late 19th century and loved again today.
  • Fern (Girl): Taken from the plant, used as a first name since the end of the 1800s.
  • Blossom (Girl): From the word for a flower in bloom, used as a name since the 19th century.

Named after famous Victorians

  • Ada (Girl): Meaning “noble”. Ada Lovelace (1815–1852), Lord Byron’s daughter, was a pioneer of computing.
  • Florence (Girl or boy): Latin for “flourishing”. The nurse Florence Nightingale made it hugely popular.
  • Bronte (Girl or boy): Made famous by the Brontë sisters, the 19th-century novelists.
  • Tennyson (Girl or boy): From a surname meaning “son of Dennis”, borne by the poet Alfred, Lord Tennyson.
